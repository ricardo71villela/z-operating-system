> **Methodological note — this guide is structured differently from the single-country guides in this series.** Real property law in the United States is overwhelmingly **state** law: there is no national land registry, no national transfer tax, no national landlord-tenant code, and closing procedure itself varies by state (attorney-required vs. title/escrow-company states). Federal law governs taxation (IRS), certain non-resident withholding rules (FIRPTA), fair housing, and — increasingly — national-security-driven restrictions on foreign ownership. This guide presents the **federal baseline** that applies everywhere, plus **three representative states — Florida, California, and New York** — chosen because they are the highest-volume markets for international buyers. **Every other state has its own rules; never assume Florida, California, or New York practice applies elsewhere without checking that specific state.**

## A. BUYING A PROPERTY

**Level: STATE** (contract and closing law), with **NATIONAL** mortgage-lending and disclosure rules (TILA-RESPA) layered on top.

**Federal baseline:** the Closing Disclosure (mandated by the federal TILA-RESPA Integrated Disclosure rule) must be provided to the buyer at least **three business days** before closing when financing is involved. Fair housing protections (Fair Housing Act) apply nationwide regardless of state.

**Closing agent — a fundamental state-by-state split:**
- **Attorney states** (roughly 12–21 states plus Washington, D.C., depending on how the category is counted — including **New York**, Connecticut, Delaware, Georgia, Maine, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, North Carolina, Rhode Island, South Carolina, Vermont, and West Virginia): a licensed attorney must be involved in the closing, whether physically present, reviewing/certifying title, or preparing documents.
- **Title/escrow states** (roughly 33 states, including **Florida** and **California**, plus most of the West and Midwest): a title company or escrow/settlement agent conducts the closing; no attorney is legally required, though a buyer may still choose to hire one for a complex transaction.
- A handful of states are **hybrid**, with attorney involvement customary but not strictly mandatory.

**Typical sequence (varies in detail by state):** offer and acceptance (not legally binding until a signed purchase contract exists) → home inspection contingency → mortgage application and appraisal → title search and title insurance commitment → final walkthrough → **closing**, where the deed is signed, funds are transferred (typically by wire, never a personal check for the closing amount), and the deed is **recorded** with the county. **Ownership transfers upon delivery and recording of the deed**, not upon signing the purchase contract.

**Timeline:** typically **30–90 days** from an accepted offer/mortgage pre-approval to closing, largely driven by mortgage underwriting timelines rather than legal process.

**Title insurance:** unlike most of the jurisdictions covered elsewhere in this series, the US relies on **private title insurance** (a one-time premium paid at closing) rather than a state-guaranteed land register, to protect against defects in the chain of title not discovered by the title search. This is a structurally distinctive feature of the US system worth explaining to buyers from land-registry countries.

**Deed of trust vs. mortgage:** some states (including California) use a deed of trust with a non-judicial foreclosure process; others (including New York and Florida) use a traditional mortgage with judicial foreclosure — a distinction relevant mainly to lenders and default scenarios, but part of the underlying state-law variation buyers should be aware exists.

**Closing costs:** typically **2–5%** of the purchase price nationally, but this varies enormously by state — Missouri and Indiana are among the cheapest (no transfer tax, no attorney requirement); **New York** is among the most expensive, partly due to mandatory attorney fees and (in NYC) mansion tax and transfer tax stacking (see Section D).

## B. SELLING A PROPERTY

**Level: STATE**

**Disclosure obligations vary significantly by state.** Most states require a seller's property disclosure statement covering known material defects; California in particular has an extensive statutory disclosure regime (Transfer Disclosure Statement, natural hazard disclosures, and more). Florida shifted toward more of a "buyer beware" default in recent years but still requires disclosure of known material defects not readily observable.

**Typical seller costs:** real estate agent commission (commonly negotiated, historically around 5–6% combined but now more variable following 2024 US industry-wide commission-structure litigation and settlements), title insurance owner's policy (often seller-paid by local custom, especially in some counties), any mortgage payoff, applicable transfer taxes (Section D), and the seller's federal (and state, where applicable) capital gains tax exposure (Section C).

## C. TAXATION OF CAPITAL GAINS

**Level: NATIONAL** (Internal Revenue Code, IRS) / **STATE** (many states also tax the gain as ordinary state income)

**Section 121 exclusion (primary residence):** a US tax resident who owned and used the home as a primary residence for at least **2 of the 5 years** before sale can exclude up to **$250,000** of gain (single filer) or **$500,000** (married filing jointly) from federal taxable income. This exclusion can be used once every 2 years. Gain above the exclusion is taxed as long-term capital gain (0%, 15%, or 20% federal rate depending on income) if the property was held over a year.

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** an additional **3.8%** federal surtax applies to investment income (including taxable real estate gains) for higher earners (Modified AGI over $200,000 single / $250,000 married filing jointly).

**Depreciation recapture:** if the property (or part of it) was ever rented out or used for business, any depreciation taken — or that **should have been** taken, whether or not actually claimed — must be recaptured at a **25%** federal rate, even where Section 121 otherwise excludes the balance of the gain. This is a frequent, easily-missed liability for owners who casually rented a room or unit.

**Foreign (non-resident) sellers — FIRPTA:** the **Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act** requires the **buyer** of US real property from a foreign person (a "non-resident alien" or foreign entity) to withhold **15% of the gross sales price** (not just the gain) and remit it to the IRS within **20 days of closing**, via Form 8288/8288-A. This is **not** the final tax — it is a payment on account; the foreign seller subsequently files Form 1040-NR to reconcile the actual tax owed and claim any refund of over-withholding. A seller can apply in advance for a **Withholding Certificate (Form 8288-B)** to reduce the withholding to the expected actual tax liability, but IRS processing can take several months, so this must be planned **well before** closing, not after. Non-resident aliens may also claim the Section 121 exclusion on their own primary residence, but — because they generally cannot file jointly unless married to a US person — the maximum excludable amount for a non-resident alien filer is **$250,000**, not $500,000.

**Important distinction:** FIRPTA applies only to **foreign persons** — it does **not** apply to US citizens or Green Card holders living abroad, who remain "US persons" for tax purposes, pay ordinary federal capital gains tax, and file a standard Form 1040 (not 1040-NR). This is a commonly confused point that should be stated clearly to any American-expatriate seller.

**State-level capital gains — example, New York:** New York taxes capital gains as ordinary state income; a **non-resident** seller of New York property must file **Form IT-2663** at closing with an estimated tax payment of **10.9%** of the gain, reconciled the following year on a nonresident return. A New York City resident seller is additionally subject to NYC resident income tax (up to 3.876%) on the gain, with no preferential city capital-gains rate. **Florida has no state income tax**, so there is no state-level capital gains tax on a Florida property sale — a material and frequently decisive difference for investors comparing states. **California** taxes capital gains as ordinary state income at California's regular income tax rates (up to 13.3% at the top bracket), with no separate preferential capital-gains rate — among the highest effective combined federal+state rates in the country for a real estate gain.

## D. TAXES ON ACQUISITION AND ON PROPERTY OWNERSHIP

**Level: STATE / LOCAL** (there is no federal transfer tax or federal annual property tax)

**At acquisition — transfer tax:** varies enormously by state, and many states have **no** transfer tax at all (e.g., Missouri, Indiana, and several others cited among the lowest-closing-cost states).
- **Florida:** documentary stamp tax on the deed, generally **$0.70 per $100** of consideration in most counties (Miami-Dade County uses a different rate structure for certain property types) — paid customarily by the seller, though this is negotiable by contract.
- **New York:** a state transfer tax plus, in **New York City**, an additional NYC Real Property Transfer Tax **and** a separate "**mansion tax**" (a graduated additional tax on residential purchases starting at $1 million, rising in steps for higher-value purchases) — New York City closing costs are consistently among the highest in the country as a result of this stacking.
- **California:** county documentary transfer tax (commonly around $1.10 per $1,000 of value, though city-level add-ons exist in some jurisdictions, e.g., San Francisco and Los Angeles have their own additional city transfer taxes on higher-value sales) — generally lower than New York's combined burden but not zero.

**Never automatically equate** any US state's transfer tax with a continental transfer tax like Spain's ITP or France's DMTO — the US has no national rate at all, and even "high-transfer-tax" states like New York are structured completely differently (city-level mansion tax stacking) from anything in the earlier guides in this series.

**Annual tax on ownership — property tax (always local/state, never federal):** this is where the US diverges most sharply from every other jurisdiction in this series, because of **assessment caps** that decouple the tax base from current market value for existing owners.
- **California — Proposition 13 (1978):** caps the property tax rate at **1%** of assessed value and limits **annual assessment increases to 2%**, regardless of market appreciation; the property is reassessed to full market value only upon **sale or new construction**. The practical effect: a home bought decades ago can carry an assessed value dramatically below a neighbor's identical home bought last year, producing large disparities in actual tax bills for equivalent properties — a distinctive feature buyers must understand, since **the tax bill resets to current market value the moment they buy**, even if the seller was paying far less.
- **Florida — "Save Our Homes":** caps annual assessment increases for **homesteaded** (owner-occupied primary residence) properties at **3% or the CPI inflation rate, whichever is lower**; non-homestead property is subject to a separate statewide assessment-growth cap. Florida also provides homestead exemptions and portability rules for qualifying primary residences. **2026 ballot measure:** **CS/HJR 1-F — Save our Homes from Excessive Property Taxes** passed both legislative chambers and was filed with the Secretary of State on 16 June 2026. If approved by voters, it takes effect on 1 January 2027; among its principal changes, it would reduce the annual assessment-growth cap for specified non-homestead real property from 10% to 5% and phase in larger non-school homestead exemptions. **LEGAL_STATUS: BALLOT_MEASURE_PENDING — current law remains controlling until voter approval and the stated effective date.**
- **New York:** no statewide assessment cap comparable to California's or Florida's — assessed value tracks market reassessments (subject to local assessment cycles, which vary by county/city), meaning tax bills can rise 10–20% year-over-year in a hot market even without any change in the tax rate itself. New York offers the **STAR (School Tax Relief)** program — Basic STAR exempts roughly $30,000 of assessed value from school tax for owners under an income threshold, with an Enhanced STAR for qualifying seniors.
- **Homestead/primary-residence exemptions generally require the owner to occupy the property as their primary US residence** — a foreign, non-resident owner of a second home or investment property in any of these three states generally does **not** qualify for the homestead exemption or the associated assessment cap protections, and will pay the un-capped, full-market-value-tracking tax bill.

**No federal or state-uniform annual wealth tax exists in the US comparable to Spain's Impuesto sobre el Patrimonio or France's IFI.** California's **Proposition 40** is on the November 2026 ballot and proposes a one-time tax of up to 5% on covered assets above $1 billion; the California Secretary of State's official ballot summary expressly states that **real property is excluded from the measure's covered assets**. It is not yet law.

## E. RESIDENTIAL TENANCIES

**Level: STATE / LOCAL** (there is no federal landlord-tenant code)

**This area varies more by state than almost any other topic in this guide.** No single federal statute governs security deposits, notice periods, or eviction grounds — each state (and often each city) sets its own rules.

- **Florida:** relatively landlord-favorable; no statutory cap on security deposits (though deposits must be held in a specific manner and interest may be owed depending on the method); eviction for non-payment can proceed relatively quickly through the courts once proper notice is given; **Florida law generally preempts local governments from enacting rent control**, with narrow exceptions.
- **California:** among the most tenant-protective states nationally. The statewide **Tenant Protection Act of 2019** caps annual rent increases (for many, though not all, rental properties) at **5% plus local CPI, up to a maximum of 10%**, and requires "just cause" for eviction after a tenant has occupied a unit for 12 months, for most covered properties. Security deposits are capped by state law. Numerous California cities (San Francisco, Los Angeles, and others) layer **additional, stricter local rent-control ordinances** on top of the state framework.
- **New York:** varies dramatically by whether a unit is **rent-stabilized** (a large share of older NYC apartment stock, subject to city Rent Guidelines Board increase limits and strong eviction protections) versus **market-rate** (subject to the general Housing Stability and Tenant Protection Act of 2019 framework, which still limits security deposits to one month's rent statewide and imposes notice-period requirements scaled by tenancy length). Rent stabilization is a NYC/downstate-specific system with no equivalent upstate.

**LEGAL_STATUS: REQUIRES_LOCAL_CONFIRMATION** — landlord-tenant law changes frequently at the state and city level in the US; the specific city/county ordinance for the property's exact location must always be checked, since rules can differ block-by-block in some metro areas (e.g., unincorporated county vs. city limits).

## F. CONDOMINIUMS, CO-OPS, AND HOMEOWNERS ASSOCIATIONS

**Level: STATE** (each state has its own condominium/common-interest-community statute)

**Terminology:** the US uses **condominium** (individual ownership of a unit plus an undivided interest in common elements, governed by a **condominium association**), **cooperative ("co-op")** (a corporation owns the building; residents own **shares** in the corporation plus a proprietary lease to their unit — common in older New York City buildings specifically, and largely absent from Florida and California markets), and **homeowners association (HOA)** (governs a planned community of individually-titled houses or townhomes, enforcing **CC&Rs** — Covenants, Conditions & Restrictions).

**Governance:** an elected board manages the association/co-op corporation, sets and collects regular **assessments** (the US equivalent of a condominium/community service charge) and, for major capital items, may levy **special assessments**. Many states now require **reserve studies** and minimum funding levels for major structural components following high-profile building-collapse incidents (notably the 2021 Champlain Towers South collapse in Surfside, Florida) that prompted **Florida** to enact new mandatory structural inspection and reserve-funding requirements for condominium buildings.

**Co-op boards (New York specifically):** unlike a condominium sale, a co-op sale requires **board approval** of the incoming purchaser — the board can reject a buyer for almost any non-discriminatory reason, and co-op boards are notorious for extensive financial-disclosure and interview requirements. This is a structurally distinctive feature with no real equivalent among Florida, California, or the other jurisdictions in this series, and buyers unfamiliar with the US market should be alerted to it specifically when considering a NYC co-op versus condo.

**Short-term rental restrictions in governing documents:** critically, **HOA, condominium, and co-op governing documents can prohibit or restrict short-term rentals even where local law permits them** — always review the specific building/community's rules (CC&Rs, bylaws, house rules) before assuming a short-term-let strategy is viable, independent of the city/county-level rules described in the module below.

## G. FOREIGN BUYER

**Level: NATIONAL** (tax treatment, FIRPTA) / **STATE** (a rapidly evolving and legally contested patchwork of ownership restrictions)

**No general federal restriction on foreign ownership of US real estate exists** — a foreign national, resident or not, can generally buy US residential property freely, and this remains true in the great majority of transactions nationwide.

**However, a fast-growing patchwork of state laws restricts ownership by nationals of specific "countries of concern"(most commonly China, and often also Russia, Iran, North Korea, Cuba, Syria, and Venezuela) — this is one of the most significant and rapidly evolving legal developments affecting foreign buyers in the US property market today, and it is genuinely contested, evenhandedly presented here:**

- As of late 2025, approximately **36 states** have enacted some form of law restricting or prohibiting foreign ownership of real property, typically focused on **agricultural land**, land **near military installations or critical infrastructure**, or (in a small number of states) more broadly.
- **Florida's SB 264 (2023)** is the most far-reaching currently in effect: it prohibits "foreign principals" connected to designated countries of concern from owning agricultural land or real property within 10 miles of a military installation or critical infrastructure facility, **and** contains a separate, narrower provision specifically restricting individuals **domiciled in the People's Republic of China** (who are not US citizens or lawful permanent residents) from owning **any** real property in Florida, subject to limited exceptions (e.g., a single primary residence up to a certain size, under specific conditions).
- **Texas's SB 17 (effective September 1, 2025)** similarly restricts direct or indirect ownership (including partial interests and leases of one year or longer) of real property — commercial, agricultural, and residential — by covered foreign individuals, entities, and governments, with an exception for a primary residence.
- Several more states enacted or amended similar laws through 2026 (including Indiana, Louisiana, Mississippi, Oklahoma, South Dakota, Tennessee, and Utah).
- **These laws are subject to active constitutional litigation.** In *Shen v. Commissioner, Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services*, the Eleventh Circuit's **4 November 2025** decision did **not** issue a merits holding broadly upholding SB 264's purchase restriction. It affirmed denial of a preliminary injunction as to the registration and affidavit requirements, but reversed and remanded as to the purchase restriction so that the motion would be denied **without prejudice because none of the plaintiffs had shown standing to challenge that provision**. The procedural result must therefore not be presented as blanket constitutional validation of the purchase restriction.
- Advocacy organizations, including the Committee of 100 and various Asian American civil rights groups, have argued these laws legitimize discriminatory treatment and contribute to anti-Asian sentiment; state legislators supporting the laws have framed them as national-security measures responding to genuine concerns about foreign-adversary land acquisition near sensitive sites. **This guide presents both positions factually without endorsing either; it does not constitute legal advice on whether a specific prospective buyer is covered by a specific state's law.**

**LEGAL_STATUS: REQUIRES_LOCAL_CONFIRMATION** — given the number of states involved, the pace of new legislation, and active litigation with uncertain outcomes, **any buyer who is a national of, or entity connected to, China, Russia, Iran, North Korea, Cuba, Syria, or Venezuela must obtain current, state-specific legal advice before purchasing property anywhere in the US**, and this applies with particular force in Florida and Texas specifically, given how comprehensive those two states' statutes are. Buyers from other countries are not currently covered by these laws in the great majority of states, but the list of covered countries and covered states continues to expand.

**CFIUS:** separately, the federal Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States may review **certain real-estate transactions by foreign persons near designated military and other sensitive installations**. Treasury's November 2024 final rule added 59 military installations to the regulatory list and expanded jurisdiction around others. This is a transaction-specific federal national-security review regime, **not a general federal prohibition on foreign ownership of agricultural or other real estate**.

**No property-linked residence route:** the US **EB-5 Immigrant Investor Program** offers a path to a green card via qualifying investment (currently requiring investment amounts in the hundreds of thousands of dollars in a qualifying job-creating enterprise), but this is **not** a simple residential-property-purchase visa — buying a personal home does not, by itself, confer any US immigration status.

## H. FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

1. **Is US property law the same in every state?** No — there is no national land registry, transfer tax, or landlord-tenant code; each state (and often each city) sets its own rules, which is why this guide covers a federal baseline plus Florida, California, and New York specifically.
2. **Do I need a lawyer to buy property in the US?** Depends on the state — roughly a dozen-plus states (including New York) require or customarily use an attorney at closing; most others (including Florida and California) let a title/escrow company handle it without a lawyer.
3. **When does ownership actually transfer?** Upon delivery and recording of the deed at closing — not upon signing the purchase contract, which (before closing) is generally not itself the moment of transfer.
4. **Do I get title insurance instead of a state land registry guarantee?** Yes — the US relies on private title insurance purchased at closing, a structurally different system from the state-guaranteed land registries used in Spain, France, Germany, Italy, Portugal, and the UK/Ireland guides in this series.
5. **Do I pay capital gains tax if I sell my only home?** Often not, up to $250,000 (single) or $500,000 (married) of gain, under the federal Section 121 exclusion, provided you owned and lived there at least 2 of the last 5 years.
6. **I'm a foreign seller — what is FIRPTA?** A federal rule requiring the buyer to withhold 15% of the gross sale price (not just the gain) and send it to the IRS, as an advance payment against your actual US tax liability, reconciled the following year.
7. **Does FIRPTA apply to me if I'm a US citizen living abroad?** No — FIRPTA applies only to foreign persons; US citizens and Green Card holders remain "US persons" for tax purposes regardless of where they live.
8. **Why did my property tax bill jump so much after I bought in California or Florida?** Both states cap annual assessment increases for existing owners (2% in California, 3%/CPI in Florida for homesteaded property), but reassess to full market value the moment a property is sold — so a new buyer's bill can be dramatically higher than the seller's had been.
9. **Does Florida have a state income tax on my capital gain?** No — Florida has no state income tax at all, so there is no additional state-level capital gains tax there, unlike New York or California.
10. **Can any foreign national buy property anywhere in the US?** In the great majority of cases yes, but a fast-growing number of states (roughly 36 as of late 2025) restrict ownership by nationals of specific countries (especially China), with Florida and Texas having the most comprehensive current restrictions — always get current, state-specific advice if this may apply to you.
11. **What is a co-op and how is it different from a condo?** A co-op (common in New York City) means you own shares in a corporation plus a lease to your unit, and the board can approve or reject any buyer; a condominium means you own your unit outright, with no board approval required to buy.
12. **Can my HOA or condo board stop me from renting my property short-term?** Yes — governing documents (CC&Rs, bylaws, house rules) can prohibit or restrict short-term rentals even where city or state law would otherwise allow it.
13. **Can I rent my New York City apartment on Airbnb?** In almost all cases, no — Local Law 18 has made it effectively illegal to rent an entire unit for under 30 days unless you are physically present throughout the stay, occupy the unit as your primary residence, and follow strict conditions (maximum 2 guests, no locks on guest bedroom doors, prior registration).
14. **Does buying US property give me a visa or green card?** No, not automatically — the EB-5 investor visa program offers an immigration path via a much larger, job-creating qualifying investment, but simply buying a personal residence confers no immigration status.
15. **Is short-term rental regulation the same in every US city?** No — it ranges from Texas/Arizona's state laws limiting local bans, to New York City's near-total prohibition on entire-unit rentals, to Hawaii's resort-zone-only restrictions — always check the specific city and county.

## I. GLOSSARY

- **Closing:** the final step of a US property transaction, where the deed is signed, funds transfer, and (usually) the deed is recorded — the US equivalent of "completion."
- **Title insurance:** a one-time-premium private insurance policy protecting against defects in the chain of title, used instead of a state-guaranteed land register.
- **Escrow:** the neutral holding of funds/documents pending completion of contract conditions, and (in escrow states) the closing process itself.
- **Deed:** the legal document transferring ownership; recording it with the county completes the transfer's public record.
- **FIRPTA:** the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act, requiring 15% buyer withholding on sales by foreign sellers.
- **Section 121 exclusion:** the federal capital-gains exclusion (up to $250,000/$500,000) for a qualifying primary residence sale.
- **NIIT (Net Investment Income Tax):** an additional 3.8% federal surtax on investment income (including some real estate gains) for higher earners.
- **Homestead exemption:** a reduction in assessed value (and, in Florida and California, an associated cap on annual assessment increases) available only to an owner-occupied primary residence.
- **Proposition 13:** California's constitutional 1% rate cap and 2%/year assessment-increase limit, reset to market value upon sale.
- **Save Our Homes:** Florida's equivalent assessment cap (3%/CPI, whichever is lower) for homesteaded property.
- **Mansion tax:** New York City's graduated additional transfer tax on residential purchases starting at $1 million.
- **Condominium association / HOA / co-op board:** the US equivalents of a community-of-owners body, each with distinct legal structures and, for co-ops, a buyer-approval power the others lack.
- **CC&Rs (Covenants, Conditions & Restrictions):** the governing rules of an HOA-managed community, which can include short-term-rental restrictions.
- **EB-5:** the US immigrant investor visa program, unrelated to ordinary residential property purchase.
- **Local Law 18:** New York City's short-term rental registration law, which has made most entire-unit short-term rentals effectively illegal in NYC.

## SEPARATE MODULE: SHORT-TERM RENTAL

**Name of the regime:** **short-term rental (STR)**, regulated almost entirely at the **state, county, and city level simultaneously — there is no federal short-term-rental law of any kind.**

**"This subject is regulated primarily at state, county, and city level, with governing-document (HOA/condo) restrictions layered on top."**

**State-level approaches vary from permissive to highly restrictive:**
- **Texas, Arizona, Idaho (new 2026), and Indiana (new 2026)** have active **STR preemption laws** that limit or prevent local governments from banning short-term rentals outright or imposing restrictive license caps — a "host-friendly" state-level approach.
- **California** has no statewide STR preemption (a 2026 preemption bill failed), leaving a fragmented, city-by-city regulatory environment with generally tightening rules in most major markets (San Francisco and Los Angeles both have significant local restrictions requiring primary-residence occupancy for most STR permits).
- **Florida** has historically favored state preemption of local STR bans for certain categories, though local registration/licensing requirements still commonly apply — always check the specific county/city.
- **Hawaii** is considered the most restrictive state overall, generally permitting short-term rentals only in resort-zoned areas.

**New York City — the most restrictive major US market (LOCAL to NYC specifically):** **Local Law 18** (enacted January 9, 2022; enforcement began September 5, 2023) requires hosts to register with the **Mayor's Office of Special Enforcement (OSE)** before listing any unit for stays under 30 days, and prohibits booking platforms from processing unregistered listings. In practice, combined with pre-existing state Multiple Dwelling Law restrictions, this has made entire-unit short-term rentals **effectively illegal** in most NYC buildings — the only realistic lawful path is a narrow "host-present" exception requiring:
- the host to be a **permanent New York State resident** who occupies the unit as their **primary residence**;
- the host to be **physically present** throughout the guest's stay;
- a maximum of **2 guests** at a time;
- **no locks** on guest bedroom doors;
- prior registration ($145 one-time fee) with the OSE before listing.

Building owners, co-op boards, and condo boards can also proactively place their building on OSE's **prohibited buildings list**, blocking registration entirely for units in that building — a buyer intending to host should check this list **before** purchasing, not after.

**Impact:** short-term listings in NYC fell from roughly 22,000 before enforcement to around 2,300 shortly after, and the city has levied over **$72 million** in cumulative STR fines as of 2026.

**Taxation (NYC-specific example):** as of March 1, 2025, New York State sales tax plus a NYC-specific occupancy fee of **$1.50 per unit per day** applies to short-term rental stays, generally collected by the booking platform.

**LEGAL_STATUS: REQUIRES_LOCAL_CONFIRMATION** — short-term rental regulation in the US changes frequently at the city and county level, and the specific rules for a property's exact municipality (which can differ from the county or even from an adjacent city) must always be verified directly with the relevant local government before any transaction or listing decision.

**HOA/condo/co-op overlay:** independent of all public-law rules above, the property's own governing documents can separately prohibit or restrict short-term rental — this is a distinct and equally binding layer described in Section F.

**Comparison with other jurisdictions in this series:** the US short-term rental landscape is the most fragmented of any jurisdiction covered so far — more fragmented even than Spain's regional-primary model — because regulation can differ not just state-by-state but genuinely city-by-city and building-by-building, with no unifying national registration layer of the kind seen (even imperfectly) in Italy, Scotland, or the France/Germany national-registration components.

## POINTS REQUIRING HUMAN REVIEW / LEGAL VALIDATION

1. **Foreign ownership restriction laws (state-by-state):** this is a fast-moving, actively litigated area; confirm the current status of the specific buyer's nationality against the specific target state's law immediately before any transaction — do not rely on this guide's snapshot.
2. **Florida CS/HJR 1-F and California Proposition 40** are November 2026 ballot measures, not current law. Recheck voter results and effective dates after the election before relying on either measure.
3. **Short-term rental regulation** at the specific city/county level: verify directly with the local government, as this changes frequently and can differ within a single metro area.
4. **State-specific landlord-tenant rules** beyond Florida/California/New York: confirm rules for any other state, since this guide does not cover all 50 states individually.
5. **Attorney-state classification:** confirm current requirements for the specific state, as the list is drawn from industry sources that "should be reviewed annually," per one cited source.
6. **CFIUS jurisdiction expansion:** confirm current scope for any transaction involving land near government/military sites or large agricultural acreage, given ongoing federal-level national-security policy development.
7. **Title, survey, and closing-agent verification** for any specific transaction; this guide does not replace that case-by-case due diligence, especially given the state-by-state variation in closing procedure described above.
