> **Methodological note — structured like the US guide in this series.** Canadian real property law is provincial: each of the 10 provinces and 3 territories has its own land registration system, land transfer tax, landlord-tenant act, and condominium/strata statute. **Quebec is a fundamentally different legal system** — a civil-law jurisdiction (Code civil du Québec) using **notaries** rather than the common-law conveyancing lawyers used in the other nine provinces, with French as the primary language of legal documents. This guide presents the **federal baseline** (capital gains tax, the federal foreign buyer ban, GST/HST) plus **three representative provinces — Ontario, British Columbia, and Quebec** — chosen for their volume of international buyers. **Every other province/territory has its own rules; never assume Ontario, BC, or Quebec practice applies elsewhere (e.g., Alberta and Saskatchewan charge no land transfer tax at all).**

## A. BUYING A PROPERTY

**Level: PROVINCIAL** (conveyancing process, land registration), with **FEDERAL** mortgage-insurance rules (CMHC) layered on top for insured mortgages.

**Common-law provinces (including Ontario and British Columbia):** a licensed real estate lawyer handles the Agreement of Purchase and Sale, conducts title searches, prepares closing documents, and registers the transfer at the provincial land title/registry office. **A lawyer is mandatory in Ontario and Alberta**; in most other common-law provinces, including BC, using a lawyer is standard practice though not always strictly compelled by statute for every step. Typical legal fees run **$1,000–$2,500** plus disbursements.

**Quebec — a structurally different system:** Quebec uses **notaries**, not conveyancing lawyers, reflecting its civil-law tradition. The notary drafts and reads the **Deed of Sale**, calculates adjustments between buyer and seller (prepaid property taxes, condo fees, special assessments, prorated to the possession date), arranges the signing appointment for both parties, registers the sale in the **Quebec Land Register**, transfers funds to the seller, and provides certified copies of the deed. **Ownership officially passes only once this registration is complete.** Quebec's land transfer tax (see Section D) is due within **90 days** of the notary updating the Land Register.

**Closing timeline and costs:** overall closing costs (legal/notarial fees, land transfer tax, title insurance, inspection, appraisal) typically run **1.5%–4%** of the purchase price nationally, though Ontario and Toronto specifically (where both provincial and municipal land transfer tax apply) tend toward the higher end, at **2.5%–4.5%**.

**Down payment requirements:** Canadian citizens and permanent residents must contribute at least **5%** on the first $500,000 of the purchase price and **10%** on the remaining balance (mortgage default insurance via CMHC required below 20% down). **Newcomers without Canadian credit history, and non-resident buyers generally, often need a 35% down payment**, since mortgage default insurance is generally unavailable to non-residents.

## B. SELLING A PROPERTY

**Level: PROVINCIAL**

Realtors' commissions are generally paid by the **seller** (typically negotiated, historically around 5% but variable). Sellers pay legal/notarial fees, mortgage discharge fees, and — critically for non-resident sellers — federal withholding obligations described in Section C.

**Quebec specifically:** the notary calculates adjustment reimbursements between buyer and seller for prepaid municipal/school taxes, condo fees, and special assessments as part of closing.

## C. TAXATION OF CAPITAL GAINS

**Level: FEDERAL** (Income Tax Act, administered by the Canada Revenue Agency — CRA; capital gains tax is not a separate provincial tax, though provincial income tax rates apply to the included portion of the gain)

**Principal Residence Exemption (PRE):** a property that was your (or your family unit's) principal residence for **every year** you owned it is **fully exempt** from capital gains tax on sale. Only **one property per family unit** may be designated as the principal residence for a given year. **The exemption is not automatic** — since 2016, the sale must be reported on your T1 return (Schedule 3) with the designation claimed, even where the full gain is exempt; failing to report can result in penalties and potential loss of the exemption. If the property was not your principal residence for the entire ownership period (e.g., it was rented out for some years), the exemption is prorated — e.g., owned 10 years, principal residence for 8, exempts 8/10 of the gain. A **Section 45(2) election** can preserve PRE status for up to 4 years even after a change in use to a rental, if filed with the return for the year the use changed.

**Inclusion rate — a genuinely unsettled recent history, now resolved:** the capital gains **inclusion rate for individuals is 50%** for 2026, confirmed unchanged. (Background: the 2024 federal budget proposed raising the inclusion rate to 66.67% on gains above $250,000/year; this was first deferred in January 2025, then **fully cancelled in March 2025**.) **LEGAL_STATUS: REQUIRES_LOCAL_CONFIRMATION** — some commercial calculator tools found in research for this guide still describe a two-tier 50%/66.67% system with a $250,000 annual threshold, which reflects the **cancelled 2024 proposal**, not current law; always verify the applicable inclusion rate against current CRA guidance at the time of any transaction, given how recently and how repeatedly this rate changed.

**Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption (LCGE):** $1,275,000 for 2026 (indexed from the $1.25 million set by the June 2024 budget), but this applies only to qualifying small business corporation shares and qualifying farm/fishing property — **not** to ordinary real estate holdings, including investment or rental properties.

**Investment/rental property (non-principal-residence) sale:** 50% of the gain is included in taxable income and taxed at the seller's marginal federal + provincial combined rate.

**Non-resident sellers — Section 116 purchaser-liability / compliance-certificate regime:** Canadian real estate is generally "taxable Canadian property." Where the statutory exceptions do not apply and no relevant compliance certificate protects the purchaser, **subsection 116(5)** can make the purchaser liable to remit **25% of the cost of the property** (or the amount by which that cost exceeds the certificate limit). For the specified property categories governed by **subsection 116(5.3)**, purchaser liability can instead be **50% of the purchase amount** (or the excess over the applicable certificate amount). **The 50% rule is category-specific and must not be reduced to a blanket "50% of the excess over UCC" formula.** Certificate type, property classification, withholding and filing should be confirmed for the actual transaction with Canadian tax counsel/accountants.

**No separate provincial capital gains tax:** unlike US states such as New York and California, Canadian provinces do not impose a separate capital-gains-specific tax; provincial income tax applies to the included (50%) portion of the gain at ordinary provincial rates as part of the taxpayer's total income.

## D. TAXES ON ACQUISITION AND ON PROPERTY OWNERSHIP

**Level: PROVINCIAL** (land transfer tax) / **MUNICIPAL** (annual property tax, and — in Toronto only — an additional municipal land transfer tax)

**At acquisition — Land Transfer Tax (LTT), by province (2026):**
- **Alberta and Saskatchewan charge NO land transfer tax** — only smaller land title registration fees (Saskatchewan's is value-based, roughly $2,000 on a $500,000 title).
- **Ontario:** tiered Land Transfer Tax; on a $500,000 home in Toronto, the combined provincial + municipal LTT is **$12,950** (Toronto is the only Canadian city that layers its own municipal LTT on top of the provincial tax). First-time buyers can receive a provincial rebate of up to **$4,000** plus a Toronto municipal rebate of up to **$4,475** — up to **$8,475** combined. New luxury LTT rates apply to Ontario homes over $3 million from April 2026.
- **British Columbia:** Property Transfer Tax (PTT), tiered **1%–3%**. First-time buyers get a **full exemption up to $500,000** (partial above); a separate **Newly Built Home Exemption** waives PTT on new construction up to **$1,100,000** (partial to $1,150,000), open to move-up buyers as well as first-timers.
- **Quebec:** "Welcome Tax" (*taxe de bienvenue* / *droits de mutation immobilière*), tiered from the provincial 0.5%–1.5% scale, with cities like Montréal adding higher-value tiers up to **4%**.

**Never automatically equate** Canadian LTT with continental transfer taxes: it is a **one-time closing cost**, paid entirely upfront in cash (it **cannot** be rolled into the mortgage), and varies so dramatically by province (from $0 in Alberta to over $12,000+ in Toronto on an identical-priced home) that the province — not just the country — is the determinative variable.

**Foreign buyer surcharges — layered on top of standard LTT where a purchase is otherwise legally permitted (see Section G for the federal ban itself):**
- **Ontario — Non-Resident Speculation Tax (NRST):** **25%** of the purchase price, province-wide, since October 25, 2022, applying to land containing 1–6 single-family residences (and, since March 2024, condo parking/storage units bought with a unit). **Toronto adds a further 10% municipal surcharge** since January 1, 2025, for a **combined 35%** in the city. A rebate is available if the buyer becomes a permanent resident within 4 years.
- **British Columbia — Additional Property Transfer Tax:** **20%**, applying in Metro Vancouver and several surrounding regional districts specifically (not province-wide, unlike Ontario's NRST).
- **Nova Scotia — Non-Resident Deed Transfer Tax:** doubled to **10%** in April 2025; notably, this applies to **any buyer who does not primarily reside in the province** — meaning it captures out-of-province Canadians as well as international buyers, a structurally different (residence-based, not nationality-based) approach from Ontario's or BC's foreign-buyer-specific taxes.
- **Alberta and New Brunswick charge no foreign buyer surcharge at all.**

**Annual property tax:** municipal, assessed on property value (assessment methodology and cycle vary by municipality/province — e.g., some Ontario assessments have remained frozen at older base-year values for extended periods pending review cycles); no province-wide uniform rate exists.

**Federal Underused Housing Tax (UHT) — REPEALED:** the 1% annual federal tax on vacant/underused residential property owned by non-resident non-Canadians was **eliminated** by **Bill C-15, which received Royal Assent on 26 March 2026** — **no UHT is payable and no return is required for 2025 and later years.** **LEGAL_STATUS: PRIMARY_OFFICIAL** — CRA confirms that affected owners do not need to file a UHT return or pay UHT for **2025 and subsequent calendar years**. Filing/payment obligations can still remain for **2022, 2023 and 2024**, so historical compliance must be checked separately.

**Municipal vacancy taxes (separate from the now-repealed federal UHT):** several cities maintain their own vacant-home taxes independent of federal policy — e.g., **Vancouver's Empty Homes Tax** at 5% (increasing in some years) and **Toronto's Vacant Home Tax** — these are unaffected by the UHT's federal repeal and must be checked at the municipal level.

## E. RESIDENTIAL TENANCIES

**Level: PROVINCIAL** (there is no federal landlord-tenant statute) — **this is the area with the widest variation across the three example provinces.**

**Ontario (Residential Tenancies Act, 2006):** rent increases for most existing tenancies are capped by an annually published **guideline** — **2.1% for 2026**. Landlords must give **90 days' written notice** using a prescribed form before an increase takes effect, and can raise rent only once every 12 months for a given tenant. Critically, **Ontario allows "vacancy decontrol"**: once a tenant moves out, the landlord can reset the rent to whatever the market will bear before the next tenancy begins — the guideline caps only apply to the rent an existing, ongoing tenant pays. **Units first occupied after November 2018 are entirely exempt** from rent control in Ontario — a significant carve-out with no equivalent in BC. Evictions follow a strict "N-form" system (e.g., N4 for unpaid rent with 14 days' notice, N12 for landlord's own use with 60 days' notice, N13 for major renovations), adjudicated by the **Landlord and Tenant Board (LTB)**.

**British Columbia (Residential Tenancy Act):** the annual rent increase limit is set each year — **2.3% for 2026** (down from 3% in 2025) — and, unlike Ontario, **applies to almost all tenancies with no new-construction carve-out**. Landlords must give a full **3 months' written notice** using the prescribed RTB-7 form, and can increase rent only once every 12 months. BC has recently strengthened compensation owed to tenants where a landlord invokes a personal-use eviction ground and then re-lists the unit at a higher rent (a bad-faith eviction protection). Disputes go to the **Residential Tenancy Branch (RTB)**.

**Quebec (Code civil du Québec, adjudicated by the Tribunal administratif du logement — TAL):** Quebec has **no single published province-wide rent cap** — instead, the TAL publishes **advisory guideline percentages by building type** each year (reflecting a formula based on municipal taxes, insurance, and major repair costs, not a simple CPI figure). **The burden shifts to the landlord**: if a tenant refuses a proposed increase above the guideline, the landlord must apply to the TAL for authorization to enforce it — many tenants successfully hold increases to the guideline simply by refusing, since the TAL is described as unusually willing to roll back contested increases. This structurally different, landlord-must-justify approach is a notable contrast with Ontario's and BC's landlord-sets/tenant-disputes model.

**LEGAL_STATUS: REQUIRES_LOCAL_CONFIRMATION** — rent increase guidelines are republished annually in each province (Ontario 2.1%, BC 2.3% cited here are 2026 figures) and should be reconfirmed for the specific year of any tenancy.

## F. CONDOMINIUMS / STRATA / COPROPRIÉTÉ

**Level: PROVINCIAL** (each province has its own statute and terminology)

- **Ontario:** governed by the **Condominium Act, 1998**, administered by a **condominium corporation** with an elected board; owners pay monthly **common expense fees** (the Ontario equivalent of a service charge) and the corporation maintains a **reserve fund** for major capital repairs, informed by a mandatory **reserve fund study** updated periodically.
- **British Columbia:** uses the term **"strata"** rather than "condominium" — governed by the **Strata Property Act**, with a **strata corporation**, elected **strata council**, monthly **strata fees**, and a mandatory **Contingency Reserve Fund (CRF)**, informed by a **depreciation report**. **Critically for short-term rental purposes: BC strata corporations can restrict or prohibit short-term rentals entirely through their bylaws**, and can fine owners up to **$1,000 per contravention per day** for violations — see the short-term rental module below for how this interacts with provincial STR law.
- **Quebec:** uses **"copropriété divise"** (divided co-ownership), governed by the Code civil du Québec, with a **syndicat de copropriété** (the owners' association) and an elected board of directors; recent Quebec reforms (following high-profile building-deterioration concerns, in a pattern echoing Florida's post-Surfside reforms) have strengthened mandatory reserve-fund contribution requirements and periodic building-condition assessment obligations.

**Common thread across all three provinces:** governing documents (condo declaration/bylaws, strata bylaws, or the syndicat's regulations) can **independently restrict or prohibit short-term rentals**, in addition to whatever municipal or provincial short-term-rental law otherwise permits — always check the specific building's governing documents before assuming a short-term-let strategy is viable.

## G. FOREIGN BUYER

**Level: FEDERAL** (the purchase ban itself) / **PROVINCIAL** (surcharges applying where a purchase is otherwise legally permitted — see Section D)

**The federal foreign buyer ban — Prohibition on the Purchase of Residential Property by Non-Canadians Act:** bars non-Canadians from directly or indirectly purchasing "restricted residential property" in **census metropolitan areas (CMAs)** and **census agglomerations (CAs)** — in practice, essentially all urban and suburban Canada, including the entire Greater Toronto Area. **Originally set to expire January 1, 2025, the ban was extended by two years to January 1, 2027** (announced February 2024).

**Who is covered:** the Act applies to individuals who are not Canadian citizens or permanent residents, **and** to corporations/entities where non-Canadians hold **10% or more** of equity or voting rights — a deliberately broad anti-avoidance threshold designed to catch indirect foreign control through corporate structures.

**Key exceptions (where a purchase remains legally permitted despite the ban):**
- Properties **outside** designated CMAs/CAs (many condos and cottages/vacation properties fall outside these boundaries and remain fully open to foreign buyers).
- **Vacant land** zoned for residential or mixed use (removed from the ban entirely by March 2023 amendments), and purchases intended for **development** (building new housing).
- Certain work-permit holders and international students meeting specific criteria (for students: enrolled at a designated learning institution, Canadian tax returns filed for each of the past 5 years, physically present in Canada at least 244 days in each of those years, and purchase price capped at $500,000).
- Spousal co-purchases with a Canadian citizen or permanent resident.
- Refugees and certain other protected categories.

**Provincial surcharges are a separate and additional layer:** as detailed in Section D, even where a purchase is legally permitted under a federal exception, provincial taxes (Ontario's 25% NRST, BC's 20% Additional PTT, Nova Scotia's 10% surcharge) still apply in full — **the purchase ban is a question of whether you can buy; the provincial tax is a separate question of how much tax you owe if you do.** A work-permit holder exempt from the federal ban may still owe 20–25% in provincial tax.

**Timing caution:** the Act applies to when the purchase is legally treated as made, not simply the closing date — a non-Canadian signing an agreement in late 2026 for a 2027 closing should not assume the ban's January 1, 2027 expiry automatically resolves their situation; this is genuinely fact-specific and requires advice before signing.

**Enforcement:** a purchase made in violation of the ban can lead to fines and a **court-ordered forced sale** of the property; real estate agents and lawyers who knowingly assist a violating transaction can also face liability.

**LEGAL_STATUS: REQUIRES_LOCAL_CONFIRMATION** — whether the federal ban is further extended, allowed to expire on schedule, or modified before January 1, 2027 should be confirmed close to the date of any transaction, given the ban has already been extended once beyond its original expiry.

**No property-linked residence route:** owning Canadian property confers **no** immigration status. Canada's investor-linked immigration pathways (e.g., certain provincial nominee programs with investment components) are unrelated to simple residential property purchase.

## H. FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

1. **Is property law the same across Canada?** No — each province has its own land transfer tax, landlord-tenant act, and condo/strata statute; Quebec is a fully separate civil-law system using notaries instead of common-law lawyers.
2. **Do I need a lawyer to buy property in Canada?** Yes in virtually all cases — mandatory in Ontario and Alberta, standard practice elsewhere; in Quebec, a notary performs the equivalent role.
3. **How much is land transfer tax?** It varies enormously by province — from $0 in Alberta/Saskatchewan (just smaller registration fees) to over $12,000 in Toronto on a $500,000 home once the municipal surcharge is added.
4. **Can foreign nationals buy property in Canada right now?** In most urban areas, generally no — the federal ban runs to January 1, 2027 — but exceptions exist for properties outside major census areas, vacant land for development, certain work/study permit holders, and spousal co-purchases.
5. **If I qualify for an exception to the ban, do I pay extra tax?** Often yes — Ontario's 25% Non-Resident Speculation Tax (35% in Toronto) and BC's 20% Additional Property Transfer Tax apply regardless of whether the federal ban itself permits your purchase.
6. **Do I pay capital gains tax on my only home?** No — the Principal Residence Exemption fully exempts the gain if it was your only/main home throughout ownership, though the sale must still be reported.
7. **What's the current capital gains inclusion rate?** 50% for individuals in 2026 — a 2024 proposal to raise it to 66.67% above $250,000/year was cancelled in March 2025, so treat any source describing that higher rate with caution.
8. **I'm a non-resident selling Canadian property — what happens?** Section 116 can make the purchaser liable to remit **25% of the property cost** where subsection **116(5)** applies and no protective compliance certificate is available; specified property categories under subsection **116(5.3)** can instead use **50%**. The purchaser may withhold the applicable amount from the purchase price. Obtain transaction-specific Canadian tax advice and address the certificate process before closing.
9. **Does the federal Underused Housing Tax still apply?** No — it was repealed by Bill C-15 (Royal Assent 26 March 2026); no UHT is payable and no return required for 2025 onward, though some 2022–2024 filing obligations may still apply retroactively.
10. **Are there still municipal vacancy taxes even though the federal UHT is gone?** Yes — Vancouver's Empty Homes Tax and Toronto's Vacant Home Tax are separate municipal measures, unaffected by the federal repeal.
11. **How much can my landlord raise my rent?** Depends entirely on the province — Ontario's 2026 guideline is 2.1% (but resets to market rate once a unit is vacated, and buildings first occupied after November 2018 are exempt entirely); BC's 2026 cap is 2.3% with no such carve-outs; Quebec has no fixed cap, only an advisory guideline the landlord must justify if a tenant objects.
12. **Can I list my investment condo on Airbnb in Toronto or Vancouver?** In most cases, no — both cities restrict short-term rentals to a host's principal residence; an investment property not lived in by the host generally cannot be short-term let.
13. **Can my condo/strata board stop me from short-term renting even if the city allows it?** Yes — condo declarations (Ontario), strata bylaws (BC, with fines up to $1,000/day), and syndicat regulations (Quebec) can independently prohibit short-term rentals regardless of municipal permission.
14. **Does buying Canadian property give me a visa or residency?** No — property ownership confers no immigration status by itself.
15. **Is short-term rental regulation the same across Canada?** No — it's a patchwork of provincial (BC's mandatory registry) and municipal (Toronto, Vancouver, Montreal principal-residence rules) requirements with no single federal framework.

## I. GLOSSARY

- **Agreement of Purchase and Sale:** the binding contract in common-law provinces, prepared/reviewed by the buyer's/seller's lawyer.
- **Deed of Sale:** the equivalent instrument in Quebec, drafted by a notary.
- **Land Transfer Tax (LTT) / Property Transfer Tax (PTT) / Welcome Tax:** the province-specific names for Canada's one-time closing-cost transfer tax (Ontario/Manitoba/New Brunswick call it LTT; BC calls it PTT; Quebec calls it the "welcome tax"/droits de mutation).
- **CMHC (Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation):** the federal Crown corporation providing mortgage default insurance for down payments under 20%.
- **Principal Residence Exemption (PRE):** the federal exemption from capital gains tax for a qualifying main home.
- **Section 116 compliance certificate:** a CRA certificate used in the non-resident disposition regime to limit purchaser exposure; subsection **116(5)** generally uses 25% of purchaser cost, while subsection **116(5.3)** uses 50% for specified property categories, subject to certificate and excluded-property rules.
- **Prohibition on the Purchase of Residential Property by Non-Canadians Act:** the federal foreign buyer ban, extended to 1 January 2027.
- **Non-Resident Speculation Tax (NRST):** Ontario's 25% provincial surcharge on non-Canadian residential buyers (35% combined in Toronto).
- **Additional Property Transfer Tax:** British Columbia's 20% surcharge on foreign buyers in designated regions including Metro Vancouver.
- **Underused Housing Tax (UHT):** the former federal 1% annual tax on vacant/underused property owned by non-resident non-Canadians, repealed by Bill C-15 (26 March 2026).
- **Condominium corporation / strata corporation / syndicat de copropriété:** the Ontario, BC, and Quebec equivalents of a community-of-owners body.
- **Landlord and Tenant Board (LTB) / Residential Tenancy Branch (RTB) / Tribunal administratif du logement (TAL):** the Ontario, BC, and Quebec tenancy dispute-resolution bodies respectively.
- **Vacancy decontrol:** the Ontario-specific rule allowing rent to reset to market rate once a unit is vacated between tenancies.

## SEPARATE MODULE: SHORT-TERM RENTAL

**Name of the regime:** **short-term rental (STR)**, legal nationwide but governed by "a patchwork of provincial and municipal rules — there is no single federal framework."

**"This subject is regulated primarily at provincial and municipal level, with condo/strata governing documents layered on top."**

**British Columbia — the most heavily regulated province:** the **Short-Term Rental Accommodations Act (STRAA)**, in force since **May 1, 2024**, established a **mandatory province-wide registry**; since **May 1, 2025**, all hosts, platforms, and strata-hotel platforms operating in BC must be registered, with the registration number displayed on every listing, and platforms required to validate registration numbers against the provincial registry. **In any BC municipality with a population over 10,000** (and in most smaller communities within 15 km of one), **only principal residences** may be listed as short-term rentals, plus one secondary suite or accessory dwelling unit where applicable — investment condos and second homes are generally excluded from short-term letting in these areas. Municipalities under 10,000 population may opt out of the principal-residence restriction. Provincial 8% PST applies to STR income. Fines are severe: municipal tickets up to **$3,000 per infraction per day**, and regional-district bylaw fines up to **$50,000**.

**Vancouver specifically (within BC's provincial framework):** a short-term rental is defined as **fewer than 90 consecutive days** (changed from the previous 30-day threshold in October 2024, to align with provincial legislation — stays of 30–89 days now fall under the STR framework rather than long-term rental rules). A host may rent their **principal residence** (where they live at least 9 months/180+ days per year, sources vary slightly on the exact figure) for up to **180 nights per year** while away, with no cap on renting individual rooms within an occupied principal residence. A City of Vancouver **business licence** is required (roughly $49/year for a non-operator, $99 for an operator, depending on source). **Strata corporations can override all other permissions** — a strata bylaw banning short-term rentals takes precedence even where the city and province would otherwise allow it, with fines up to $1,000/contravention/day available to the strata.

**Toronto:** short-term rentals (defined as under 28 consecutive nights) are restricted to a host's **principal residence** — an investment condo or house cannot legally be Airbnb'd for stays under 28 days; options for non-principal-residence properties are long-term rental, mid-term rental (28+ days), or living in the property and renting individual rooms. A **Short-Term Rental Operator Permit** is required, and hosts are capped at accumulating a maximum of **180 nights** of short-term bookings per calendar year (resetting each January 1) even within their own principal residence. Only **one licence per person** is permitted, tied to the address on the host's driver's licence — the city cross-references driver's licence and property tax mailing-address data weekly to detect violations. A 4% **Municipal Accommodation Tax (MAT)** applies, generally collected by platforms but still requiring quarterly host filing. Fines range from **$1,000 to $100,000 per offence** under the updated bylaw. **Condo boards may separately prohibit STR activity entirely, even where the city permits it** — always check the condo declaration first.

**Quebec/Montreal:** hosts must register with the **Corporation de l'industrie touristique du Québec (CITQ)** and obtain a classification number, which must appear on all listings and advertising; most boroughs require the listing to be the host's **principal residence**, with some tourist-zone exceptions. Quebec's **3.5% lodging tax** (Lodging Tax) applies, generally collected by platforms.

**Federal tax layer (applies everywhere):** since 2024, CRA **denies tax deductions** for non-compliant STR income (i.e., income earned in violation of applicable provincial/municipal law cannot be offset by expense deductions); since 2025, platforms **automatically report host income to CRA** under OECD DAC7-aligned rules enacted through Bill C-47. Federally, hosts must collect **GST/HST** once total taxable supplies exceed **$30,000 annually** (Airbnb now collects this automatically on behalf of many hosts).

**Mortgage and insurance considerations (independent of public-law rules):** short-term rental use can breach standard mortgage covenants, and standard homeowner/landlord insurance policies may not cover STR-related claims if the activity was not properly disclosed to the insurer.

**Comparison with other jurisdictions in this series:** British Columbia's provincial registry (STRAA) is the closest thing in this series to Italy's genuinely national CIN system or Scotland's mandatory licence — but it is **provincial, not national** (Ontario and Quebec have no equivalent province-wide registry), reinforcing that Canada's overall short-term rental landscape remains as fragmented as the United States', organized around principal-residence requirements in the country's largest cities rather than any unifying federal framework.

## POINTS REQUIRING HUMAN REVIEW / LEGAL VALIDATION

1. **Federal Underused Housing Tax repeal (Bill C-15):** confirm current status directly with CRA before advising any client, given how recent this reversal is and how many sources still describe the UHT as active.
2. **Federal foreign buyer ban expiry/extension:** confirm whether the ban is extended again, allowed to expire on 1 January 2027, or modified, close to the date of any transaction.
3. **Capital gains inclusion rate:** confirm 50% remains current, given the volatile 2024–2025 history (proposed increase to 66.67%, deferred, then cancelled) and the risk of relying on outdated calculator tools.
4. **Annual rent increase guidelines** (Ontario, BC) are republished each year — reconfirm the applicable year's figure for any tenancy.
5. **Provincial foreign-buyer surcharge rates and geographic scope** (BC's Additional PTT designated regions, in particular) should be reconfirmed, as these have changed materially over time (e.g., Ontario NRST rose from 15% to 25% in stages).
6. **Short-term rental municipal rules** for the specific city (night caps, licence fees, MAT/lodging tax rates) change frequently and should be verified directly with the municipality.
7. **Legal/notarial title verification** for any specific transaction; this guide does not replace that case-by-case due diligence, particularly given the structural difference in Quebec's process.
